# Stage 3: monetization and player psychology, within the rules

## What this stage decides

How this game earns money from this audience in these regions, which human motives it leans on, and where it refuses to go. The economy stage then makes the numbers true.

## What the template gives you

Check the store, offers, pass, daily and economy data files in this stage's context; they win over this page.

- A premium currency sold in packs, a small low-price entry product, a pre-paid stipend, and time-limited bundles of currency and resources.
- A season pass with a free track and a premium track.
- A rule-driven offers engine: triggers, timers, limits per session, account-age gates, never for minors or unknown ages.
- Time skips and **top-ups** that buy exactly the resources one action is missing; there is no free-standing currency exchange.
- Cosmetics for premium currency.
- Free premium currency from play: achievements, leagues, the daily reward, the pass's free track.
- Server-side receipt checks, an atomic under-18 monthly cap, restore, refunds taken back.
- Experiment arms that may change offer timing only (the server replays held villages with one set of numbers).

## Method

1. **Who pays, and for what feeling.** From the market record: what does this player spend on games now, by what payment method, at what price points? Time saved, self-expression, belonging and completion are the things this chassis can sell.
2. **Product ladder against the template's slots.** Each product has a job: entry (first purchase, low regret), habit (pass, stipend), value (packs with a visible anchor), moment (bundles tied to a real moment in play). Keep the template's product ids; names and contents change.
3. **Do the sums.** Free premium currency per month for a daily player against the smallest and mid packs. What a spender gets per dollar from each product: no product may make the others pointless. What skipping the longest timer costs in money. Whether a payer who rushes ends up worse off (doctrine 6).
4. **Check each offer can fire** for the players it is meant for: account age, declared age, online or offline, and whether another offer takes its slot first.
5. **Sinks.** Premium currency needs places to go that feel good: builders, cosmetics, progression bundles tied to milestones.
6. **Motives, honestly.** For each motive the design uses, name the mechanic and why it is fair:
   - mastery: a planned attack beats a big one; visible skill growth;
   - completion and collection: a roster and a codex with a clear next unlock;
   - anticipation: honest timers, a calendar, events with real ends;
   - endowed progress and goal gradient: a dressed starting base, a next-objective chip;
   - belonging and reciprocity: alliances, donations, shared wars;
   - loss aversion: raids and shields are the genre, but no invented urgency, and losses are capped.
   The test for each: would the player, shown exactly how it works, still feel respected?
7. **Never list.** Paid randomness; fake scarcity or fake timers; fake near-misses; confirm-shaming; offers that open by themselves for minors or unknown ages; obscured real-money cost; drip pricing; pay-to-skip that makes the next step worse; selling what the doctrine says is never sold.
8. **Regions.** For each launch region, the rule that shapes the design and its effect. Typical ones to confirm in the research notes and mark for re-reading: EU consumer-protection principles on virtual currencies (real-money equivalents, no forced over-buying, withdrawal information) and the unfair-practices rules; UK children's design code; US dark-pattern enforcement and children's privacy law; Japan's prepaid-payment and commerce-notice law and its ban on complete-the-set random prizes; Korea's probability disclosure; loot-box positions in Belgium, the Netherlands, Australia and Brazil; the stores' own rules on in-app purchase, odds disclosure and external payment links.
9. **Regional pricing.** Store price tiers per region, and a policy for low-income markets (lower tiers, smaller packs) so the entry product is a real entry.
10. **Experiments** for after launch: hypothesis, the single change, the metric. Offer timing only.

## What went wrong last time

- Free premium currency per month roughly equalled the mid pack; the stipend gave three times the value per dollar of any pack; skips cost about twice the genre norm.
- The first-purchase offer was a one-shot that was usually lost to another offer or an age gate.
- Offers existed only for players who had already declared themselves adults, and age was asked only at purchase.
- Rushing the main building was the cheapest large purchase and made the buyer's game worse.
- A passive weekly reward paid premium currency without play, for every missed week.
- Prices were shown as fixed dollars instead of what the store returns.
- Teens see no offers at all: model that in any forecast.

## Limits

Doctrine 5, 6, 7 and 8 in full. Forecasts are hypotheses; label them. Anything that needs legal review is an owner task, and the record says "draft, not legal advice" where it matters.

## A good record

Someone could build the store from it: each product slot, its price tier, contents and purpose; the pass; the offer rules; the sums showing packs stay worth buying; the never list; the rule per region; and a short list of requirements for the economy stage.
